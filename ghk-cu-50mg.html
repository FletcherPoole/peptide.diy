GHK-Cu 50mg Dosage Protocol

Copper tripeptide for collagen support, skin remodeling, and anti-aging research. Injectable and topical routes.

Research purposes onlyThis protocol is educational. GHK-Cu is widely sold as a research chemical and cosmetic ingredient. It is not FDA-approved as an injectable drug. This is not medical advice.

What GHK-Cu Is

GHK-Cu (glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine copper) is a naturally occurring tripeptide that binds copper(II). It was first isolated from human plasma by Loren Pickart in 1973 and declines with age. The copper is functional, not decorative: it supports lysyl oxidase and other copper-dependent enzymes involved in collagen and elastin cross-linking.

Most research focuses on dermal remodeling: fibroblast activity, collagen I/III production, elastin, glycosaminoglycans, and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) balance. Animal wound models, human fibroblast studies, and multiple topical cosmetic trials support these effects. Injectable use in humans is less formally studied than topical application, but follows the same reconstitution logic as other lyophilized peptides.

What the Research Shows

Collagen and wound repair

In rat wound-chamber models, GHK-Cu increased dry weight, DNA, total protein, collagen, and glycosaminoglycan content in a dose-dependent way. Type I and III collagen mRNAs rose; a control tripeptide did not produce the same effect (Maquart et al., 1993). Earlier fibroblast work showed direct stimulation of collagen synthesis in culture (Maquart et al., 1988).

Skin aging and topical use

Multiple human topical studies report improved skin density, firmness, laxity, and wrinkle appearance over 8–12 weeks. In a one-month thigh study comparing topical GHK-Cu to vitamin C and retinoic acid, 70% of GHK-Cu-treated subjects showed increased collagen vs 50% (vitamin C) and 40% (retinoic acid) (Abdulghani et al., 1998). Twelve-week facial cream data in photoaged skin reported reduced fine lines, improved clarity, and increased skin thickness (summarized in Pickart & Margolina, 2018, citing Leyden et al., 2002 AAD proceedings).

A randomized double-blind split-face trial using a nano-carrier GHK-Cu formulation reported significant reductions in wrinkle volume and depth vs vehicle (Canfield et al., 2017).

Gene regulation and broader repair

Review-level work describes GHK modulating large sets of human genes toward repair and away from inflammatory/destructive expression patterns, with documented effects on fibroblasts, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling across skin, lung, and other models (Pickart et al., 2015; Pickart & Margolina, 2018).

Reconstitution (50mg vial)

Recommended BAC water: 2 mL

50 mg ÷ 2 mL = 25 mg/mL

  1. Swab the rubber stoppers on the GHK-Cu vial and BAC vial. Let dry.
  2. Draw 2 mL bacteriostatic water (1 mL at a time if using a 1 mL syringe).
  3. Inject slowly down the inside wall of the peptide vial. Do not shoot directly onto the powder.
  4. Swirl gently until clear. Do not shake.
  5. Label with date and BAC volume. Refrigerate immediately.

With BAC, expect roughly 25–30 days stability refrigerated after reconstitution. See the Storage Guide.

Inject subcutaneously (belly, thigh, or upper arm). Rotate sites. Use a fresh needle each time; do not reuse.

Suggested cycle (anecdotal): 8–12 weeks on, then 4 weeks off. Topical research trials often run 12 weeks; that length is a reasonable reference point for skin-focused goals.

Topical Use

Topical GHK-Cu has the strongest published human data. Two practical approaches:

Moisturiser mix (reconstituted peptide)

Reconstitute as above, then blend the liquid into a plain, fragrance-free moisturiser. Apply to target areas 1–2× daily. Full mixing guidance: Deployment Methods · Topical and the Calculator (topical mode).

Serum and soap (anecdotal)

Community reports often use dedicated GHK-Cu face serum for facial skin and GHK-Cu soap or body products for larger areas. Apply 1–2× daily to the areas you care about.

Microneedling (anecdotal): Some users microneedle target skin 1–2× per week (or as tolerated) to increase penetration. A pen-style device is generally preferred over stamps or rollers in community discussion. Sterility matters if skin is broken.

Combining injectable + topical (anecdotal): Many users run injectable GHK-Cu for systemic/inside-out effects while using topical serum or moisturiser mix for local skin. Community consensus treats this as complementary, not redundant.

Blends and Stacks

GHK-Cu appears in pre-mixed research blends such as GLOW (GHK-Cu + BPC-157 + TB-500) and KLOW (GHK-Cu + BPC-157 + KPV + TB-500). See Blends and Stacks for coordinated multi-compound schedules.

Common combination targets (anecdotal, not vendor-specific):

  • Anti-aging / skin: Epithalon, glutathione, MOTS-c, NAD+, SS-31, Humanin, methylene blue (research context only)
  • Hair: minoxidil, finasteride, dutasteride, ketoconazole, RU-58841, topical androgen modulators
  • Skin support: HGH (separate protocol; different legal and safety profile)

Every added compound brings interaction and side-effect risk. Start one thing at a time when possible.

AHK-Cu vs GHK-Cu

AHK-Cu (alanine-histidine-lysine copper) is a related copper peptide marketed for hair-focused research. Reported results vary by individual; some users try both and keep whichever produces better hair or skin response. Direct head-to-head clinical data are limited compared with GHK-Cu.

Copper, Zinc, and Safety

Zinc balance (anecdotal)

Copper and zinc compete for absorption. A commonly cited rough ratio is about 1:10 copper to zinc in overall intake. If you increase GHK-Cu dose, some users add zinc supplementation and watch for signs of imbalance. This is community practice, not a clinical recommendation.

Copper toxicity

Copper is essential but toxic in excess. Acute copper poisoning from peptide doses at the levels above is not supported by the math: even a 10 mg GHK-Cu injection delivers roughly 1–2 mg of elemental copper, far below published toxic thresholds for oral copper salts in animal models (ATSDR Copper ToxProfile). Still, more is not better. Stay within researched or commonly reported ranges.

Side effects

Injectable: injection-site redness, mild irritation. Topical: occasional irritation or breakouts in sensitive skin. Stop if you get persistent reactions. GHK-Cu is widely used in cosmetics; injectable route has less formal safety data.
